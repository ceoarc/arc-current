Two facilities for assessing used EV batteries opened two days apart in North Gyeongsang Province (Gyeongbuk), in southeastern Korea. On October 26, an "EV Battery Automated Assessment Center" opened in the Pohang Blue Valley National Industrial Complex. On October 28, a "BaaS (Battery as a Service) Test and Demonstration Center" was completed in the Gumi National Industrial Complex, alongside a new regional hub of the Korea Testing & Research Institute (KTR).

Both centers say their job is to measure what a used battery has left and decide where it goes next. The rules that are supposed to define that decision do not exist yet. Korea's Used Battery Act takes effect in May 2027, and an amended Motor Vehicle Management Act sets up a separate track for remanufactured EV batteries from July 2027. Both laws leave the technical criteria to enforcement decrees and ministerial rules, and none of those has been put out for public comment. Below, we separate what the institutions announced, what we could verify, and what remains unknown.

## Pohang: grading, under the climate ministry

According to the announcements, the Pohang center was built by the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment together with Gyeongbuk Province and Pohang City. It diagnoses the performance and remaining life of used batteries at module and pack level and grades them for reuse or recycling. Intake, storage, testing and grading are meant to run in tandem with Gyeongbuk's collection hub for end-of-life EV batteries, part of a nationwide network the climate ministry set up for batteries returned from subsidized vehicles.

The stated capacity is up to 150 batteries tested at once and more than 30,000 a year, compared with one or two a day under manual testing. That is what the equipment can handle at most, not what will actually arrive or what has been processed. Reports from the May 2025 groundbreaking put the budget at KRW 33.4 billion (KRW 10 billion national, KRW 5.9 billion provincial, KRW 17.5 billion city), named the Gyeongbuk Technopark as operator and targeted completion by the end of 2025. The center opened about ten months later than that. Opening-day coverage did not mention the operator again.

## Gumi: testing and demonstration, under the trade ministry

The Gumi center was built under a 2023–2027 project to expand battery usability through BaaS demonstration infrastructure with KTR, a testing institute under the Ministry of Trade and Industry, as the lead agency. Trade Minister Kim Jung-kwan attended the ceremony. The 24,576 m² site holds five new buildings and one refurbished one: a data center, plus halls for disassembly and storage, performance and safety testing, special vibration testing and field validation. The announced functions are disassembly and initial diagnosis, electrical safety and performance testing, vibration, shock and electromagnetic testing, and support for demonstrating energy storage systems (ESS) built from reused batteries. KTR also plans a KRW 23.4 billion AI-based integrated support center for reused batteries by 2029.

Reports give two budget figures. Coverage based on the city's numbers says KRW 27.2 billion (KRW 10 billion national, KRW 4.5 billion provincial, KRW 10.5 billion city, KRW 2.2 billion private). Coverage based on KTR's numbers says KRW 40.4 billion (KRW 10 billion national, KRW 15 billion local, KRW 15.4 billion from KTR). The public money is identical in both: KRW 25 billion. The gap is entirely in the non-public share, KRW 2.2 billion of private funding in the project tally versus KRW 15.4 billion of KTR's own investment in the institute's tally. Whether the larger figure also covers other items, such as the new regional hub, is not stated in any report. KTR is also one of only four bodies in Korea designated to inspect complete reused-battery systems under KC 10031, the national safety inspection standard for reused batteries ([our look at the designation record](https://current.arc.ai.kr/en/posts/reused-battery-inspection-body-2026-3/)). The announcements do not say whether that inspection will be done at the Gumi center.

When we [covered Gumi's battery task force in August](https://current.arc.ai.kr/en/posts/gumi-secondary-battery-taskforce-testbed/), the equipment and budget behind the city's BaaS plan had not been disclosed. The center now exists.

## The division of roles is a plan, not yet a practice

"Pohang grades, Gumi tests and demonstrates" is how the two centers' stated functions read side by side. We found no announcement that actually divides volumes or tasks between them. In fact, the Gumi center is also described as diagnosing remaining performance and safety and sorting batteries by usability. Some regional outlets frame it differently again, with Pohang for materials and Gumi for demonstration. Both centers have only just opened, so how the work is really split will show only in operating records.

## Graded against what?

The practical question is what standard a battery is graded against if either center is running today. Neither center has published its grading criteria in its announcements, and none of the coverage reports them.

There is one earlier reference point. According to 2023 reporting, the collection hubs run by the Korea Environment Corporation sorted batteries by remaining capacity measured through a full charge-discharge cycle: above 60% went to reuse, below 60% to recycling. The reports did not say which regulation, if any, set that 60% line, and we could not confirm whether the Pohang center uses it.

The legal standard is still blank. The Used Battery Act routes performance assessment of EV batteries to the pre-removal assessment procedure in the Motor Vehicle Management Act, whose details are left to a transport ministry ordinance. For batteries in other energy storage products designated by decree, assessment follows a trade ministry ordinance ([how the Used Battery Act relates to existing certifications](https://current.arc.ai.kr/en/posts/used-battery-act-relation-to-existing-certifications/)). On October 29, we searched the government's legislative notice portal: no notice since April 2026 with "used" (사용후) or "battery" in the title, closed notices included. The one notice under the Motor Vehicle Management Act since October 6 concerns penalties for vehicle inspection violations. Whether either center will be designated to carry out the statutory assessment has not been decided.

## Reproducibility needs more than a building

At an October 1 seminar at the National Assembly, reproducibility was named the first hurdle: the same battery under the same conditions should get the same result ([issues raised at the seminar](https://current.arc.ai.kr/en/posts/used-battery-assessment-seminar-issues/)). Automated lines help keep test conditions steady within one facility. Agreement between facilities is a different matter. It needs common rules for state of charge, temperature, current rate and the grade boundaries themselves. We covered how those conditions move the numbers in [SOH estimation methods and where they break down in the field](https://current.arc.ai.kr/en/posts/soh-estimation-methods-field-limits/). With two centers funded through different ministries now standing in the same province, whether a given battery would receive the same grade at both is a question the pending rules will have to answer.

## Where graded batteries might go

There was movement on the buyer side too. On October 15, Korea Southern Power, a state-owned generator, began a pilot in Yeongwol, Gangwon Province, building an ESS from batteries taken out of retired electric buses. It describes the system as fire-preventive, with detailed diagnostics, off-gas detection, early fire-sign monitoring and suppression. On October 14, recycler Battery Solutions said it would disassemble used LFP packs, assess their remaining performance and remanufacture those that pass into ESS units, starting with Southeast Asia and India. Both are announcements of a launch or a plan, and neither has said what criteria it uses to select batteries.

## What to watch

Building the assessment capacity before the rules arrive is not odd in itself. When the rules land, the equipment needs to be ready. Until then, though, the grades these two centers assign have no statutory basis. ARC Current will follow whether the centers publish their grading criteria and operating figures, and how the forthcoming rules set designation requirements and test conditions for assessment bodies.
